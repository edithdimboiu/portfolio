Stepping into the world of web development after years as a Certified Public Accountant felt like learning to walk again. When you’ve already built a stable career—where you know what to do, how to do it, and can quickly find answers—starting over in a new field can feel far outside your comfort zone. Honestly, it’s still a journey, but one that has turned out to be unexpectedly rewarding.

One of the first questions I asked myself was: _What does it actually mean to be a “good” developer?_ After all, we all want to be good in our area of expertise. And just as accounting is about much more than recording invoices and transactions, I’ve discovered that programming is also about far more than simply writing lines of code.

### Should I Study or Just Build?

Given my background, I was always used to paying attention to details—because details make all the difference, especially when money is involved. Naturally, my first instinct was to study everything before writing a single line of code.

But then I kept reading advice like: _“Just build stuff!”_ and _“Don’t get stuck in tutorial hell!”_ That was confusing. I eventually realized that just watching tutorials gives you the impression that you understand, but if you wait until you’ve “finished” studying a topic before practicing, you’ll likely forget most of it. So I took the leap and started building projects. Some followed tutorials, while others began from a simple idea.

Of course, this way of learning is slower. You spend more time researching, googling and adjusting. You constantly add new features and change your approach as you go. But in the end, you learn much more deeply. For me, the answer to that question is that a good developer keeps building, but also takes the time to dive deeper whenever curiosity or challenges demand it.

### Chasing Every Shiny New Framework

Technology evolves incredibly fast. Every time you open YouTube or Reddit, someone is recommending a new framework, a new feature, or these days, a new AI tool. It’s easy to feel terrified of being left behind.

But in reality, most companies run on stable, well-maintained, slightly older versions of frameworks. Stability usually matters more than novelty. That’s why my approach is to focus on solving problems and mastering the fundamentals. At the same time, I stay aware of new trends without obsessing over them. Building a strong foundation makes it much easier to add new tools or technologies later, when a job or project requires them.

### More Than Just Code

The biggest surprise for me was how much my “non-technical” background in accounting actually prepared me for development. Both fields require strong problem-solving skills—debugging code feels a lot like tracing discrepancies in a balance sheet.

Communication also matters more than I expected. In accounting, I often had to explain complex reports to clients in plain language. The same is true in software development: you might be skilled technically, but if you can’t explain things clearly to a CEO or non-technical manager, you’ll quickly run into problems.

And maybe the greatest lesson is that learning never ends. Sometimes you’ll know exactly what to do, but often you’ll face situations where the best response is admitting: _“I don’t know yet, but I’ll research it.”_ Staying humble, curious, and eager to learn is essential.

### The Journey Continues

Switching careers was daunting, but incredibly rewarding. What I’ve learned is that being a "good" developer isn’t about innate genius or memorizing every line of documentation. It’s about building a solid, adaptable foundation, balancing fundamentals with hands-on practice, staying pragmatic with tools, and bringing strong problem-solving and communication skills to the table.

For me, the most exciting part is knowing the journey never really ends.
