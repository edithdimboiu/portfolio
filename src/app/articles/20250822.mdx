When I transitioned from accounting to programming, one concept immediately stood
out to me: **technical debt**. The term sounded familiar and I quickly realized
the powerful parallels were more than just a coincidence.

### What Is Technical Debt?

The term was introduced by **Ward Cunningham** (one of the authors of the Agile Manifesto) and refers to the trade-offs we make when choosing quick solutions over long-term quality. These shortcuts are "debts" that must be "repaid" later through refactoring and maintenance.

### Types of Technical Debt

Beyond the well-known concept of code debt, research shows that technical debt can manifest in many forms, each with its own "interest" to pay. The [IBM article](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/technical-debt) highlights several key categories:

- **Architectural Debt:** When the overall system design is flawed, leading to a fragile and complex system.
- **Infrastructure Debt:** When outdated tools or a lack of automation slows down development.
- **Process Debt:** When inefficient workflows and a lack of documentation hinder team collaboration.
- **Security Debt:** When security is not prioritized from the beginning, creating vulnerabilities that are expensive to fix later.

### Is It Always Bad?

From an accountant's perspective, the answer is no. Not all debt is inherently bad. In business, companies often use debt strategically to invest and grow. Similarly, software teams may deliberately take on technical debt to:

- **Meet a critical deadline:** Sometimes, shipping a feature fast is more valuable than writing perfect code.
- **Launch a prototype:** Just like testing a new financial model, building a quick prototype helps validate an idea and secure investment.
- **Accommodate limited resources:** When time and skills are limited, teams may accept a short-term shortcut.

In other words, taking on technical debt isn't always reckless. Sometimes, it's a strategic business decision. The key isn't to avoid it completely, but to manage it proactively and with awareness.

### The Compounding Costs

My accounting background makes me particularly sensitive to hidden costs. In both fields, problems can be obscured until they become unmanageable. The real danger comes from unmanaged debt, which incurs compounding "interest" that impacts the entire project.

#### The Hidden Cost of Time

Technical debt isn't just a theoretical concept. Recent studies show it has a significant, quantifiable cost. The [Stripe report](https://stripe.com/files/reports/the-developer-coefficient.pdf) found that developers spend on average 13.5 hours per week dealing with technical debt.

According to the report, this inefficiency is responsible for an annual global GDP loss of nearly $300 billion. Additionally, [Accenture](https://www.accenture.com/us-en/insights/what-is-tech-debt) estimates that in the U.S. alone, tech debt costs $2.41 trillion a year and would require $1.52 trillion to fix.

For me, these figures are like liabilities carried off the balance sheet. The risk
remains hidden until it's too large to ignore.

### Managing the Liability

Just as a company's balance sheet tracks liabilities, an engineering team needs a similar framework for visibility and accountability. The discipline I learned in finance applies directly to development:

- **Track decisions:** Write down shortcuts and the reasoning behind them.
- **Plan repayment:** Add refactoring into the roadmap; don't just hope it happens.
- **Measure impact:** Use metrics like code complexity and bug frequency to track the "interest payments."
- **Translate for business:** Explain debt in terms of business risks, like costs and delivery delays, so stakeholders understand the full impact.

Technical debt is a type of technical risk and as long as you are aware of it, you can track it, quantify it and prioritize it by its impact.

### A Bridge Between Two Worlds

As a career switcher, I find the financial metaphor for technical debt to be a powerful mental model. It’s a reminder that both finance and programming are about **strategic trade-offs, accountability,** and **long-term consequences**. Being a good developer isn't just about writing new features - it's about recognizing when today's shortcut could become tomorrow's liability.

### The Role of AI in Tech Debt

According to [Accenture's research](https://www.accenture.com/us-en/insights/what-is-tech-debt), AI is now a leading contributor to this issue. Given the rapid growth potential of AI-related debt, companies must proactively address it. Interestingly, the same technologies that are creating this debt—AI and generative AI—are also becoming powerful tools for managing it, by accelerating development and identifying redundant code.

## Conclusion

Technical debt is a complex subject that could fill an entire conference, so it can’t be debated in a single article. However, as a conclusion, I would say that there is no "good" or "bad" way to handle technical debt: it **depends on your goals**. Recognizing and managing those obligations is what separates a sustainable system from a fragile one.
